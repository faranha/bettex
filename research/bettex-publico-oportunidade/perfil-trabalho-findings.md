## KeyFacts

- **ILO’s 25% is occupational exposure, not a forecast of job losses.** Its May 2025 GenAI update estimates that one in four workers globally are in occupations with some exposure; only 3.3% of global employment falls in its highest-exposure gradient. The ILO says transformation is more likely than full redundancy because human input remains necessary. The estimate scores nearly 30,000 tasks at six-digit occupational detail and combines worker evaluation, expert validation and AI-assisted scoring. It is theoretical exposure, not observed displacement, and does not account fully for adoption costs, infrastructure or skills. [ILO 2025 brief](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2025-05/Research%20brief_GenAI%202025%20Update.pdf); `research/bettex-publico-oportunidade/sources/perfil-trabalho-01-ilo-2025-brief.md`

- **IMF’s widely quoted 40% is a different, older measure—not directly comparable to ILO’s GenAI-only 25%.** The IMF’s January 2024 paper estimates almost 40% of global employment exposed to AI; its breakdown is 60% in advanced economies, 40% in emerging markets and 26% in low-income countries. Exposure means overlap between AI applications and occupational abilities; it does not imply replacement. The paper’s modeled impact analysis has substantial assumptions (including static sector/task structure and cross-country occupational homogeneity) and is not a measured count of jobs already changed. [IMF, 2024](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/sdn/2024/english/sdnea2024001.pdf); `research/bettex-publico-oportunidade/sources/perfil-trabalho-03-imf.md`

- **Latin America: digital access can constrain gains as well as shape exposure.** The joint ILO–World Bank Working Paper 121 uses harmonized household/labor surveys for 16 countries (about 900,000 observations; country data years vary from 2014–2022), including Brazil. It estimates 2–5% automation potential and says digital-access gaps impede productivity gains for jobs that could be augmented; it estimates about 17 million such jobs regionally. These are modeled potentials, not realized productivity gains or current Brazilian job counts. The paper also uses imputation for access and excludes general-equilibrium/second-order effects. [ILO/World Bank WP121](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2024-07/WP121_web.pdf); `research/bettex-publico-oportunidade/sources/perfil-trabalho-02-ilo-worldbank-lac.md`

- **Brazil-specific caution:** an April 2026 IPEA CTS page summarizing a chapter by Luis Kubota says Brazil is not among the countries most potentially affected by GenAI in the short term, mitigating mass-unemployment risk. It provides no quantitative estimate and describes synthesis of recent studies, not new primary labor-market measurements by IPEA. [IPEA CTS, 2026](https://www.ipea.gov.br/cts/pt/central-de-conteudo/todas-as-publicacoes/publicacoes/538-ia-e-o-futuro-do-mercado-de-trabalho); `research/bettex-publico-oportunidade/sources/perfil-trabalho-05-ipea-ia-trabalho.md`

- **Non-alarmist opportunity framing:** describe AI as a possible tool for changing tasks, productivity and access—whose benefits depend on skills and digital infrastructure—not as proof that a person must invest to remain employable or financially secure. Use of AI to manage/analyze strategies is conceptually separate from buying an AI company’s equity or a diversified portfolio. For BETTEX, the supplied project context says its token represents single-company profit rights but not equity or NAV redemption; explain those rights plainly, do not imply AI exposure equals ownership or investment upside. No source here validates the project’s hypothesis about frequent small withdrawals or links job anxiety to investor behavior. Avoid income, savings, or job-loss promises.
